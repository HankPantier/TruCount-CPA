A client called our Brookings office last spring, half-laughing, half-serious: "My Border Collie logs more hours on this farm than I do. Can I write her off?" It's a fair question, and one we hear from farmers, kennel owners, and small business owners more often than you'd think. The honest answer is almost always no, unless the dog is genuinely working in a business. There are a handful of narrow, IRS-recognized situations where dog-related costs are legitimate deductions, and knowing the difference can save you from an audit letter down the road.

## Most dogs are a personal expense, not a deduction

The IRS treats pet expenses the same way it treats groceries or a haircut: personal, nondeductible, no exceptions for how much you love the animal. Food, grooming, vet visits, boarding, even the vet bill after your Lab decides to eat a sock, none of it counts as a business or medical expense just because the dog lives at your house or rides along in your truck.

[IRS Publication 535](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p535) sets the standard for any business expense: it has to be ordinary and necessary for carrying on your trade or business. A family pet doesn't meet that bar. Neither does the office dog who mostly naps under a desk and greets clients. Warmth and morale are real benefits, but the IRS doesn't accept "good for team spirit" as a deduction category. The dog has to be doing actual, documented work that the business depends on.

## When a farm or business dog earns a real write-off

This is where it gets interesting for our ag clients. A livestock guardian dog patrolling a pasture of 60 head of cattle outside Brookings isn't a pet in the eyes of the IRS, it's working equipment, provided the farm operates as a genuine for-profit business and files Schedule F. The same logic applies to a guard dog protecting inventory at a shop, a warehouse, or an ag supply yard, and to dogs used in a legitimate breeding operation run with the intent to profit, not a hobby litter sold to neighbors once a year.

In each of these cases, the deductible costs are the ordinary and necessary ones tied to the dog's job: food, veterinary care, training, and boarding while the animal is working. If the dog splits time between guarding the henhouse and sleeping on your kids' beds, you can only deduct the business-use portion, and you need records that back up that split. ## Service animals get a different deduction entirely

A service animal isn't a business expense at all, it's a medical expense, and it lives on a completely different part of your return. [IRS Publication 502](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502) allows you to deduct the cost of buying, training, and maintaining a guide dog or other service animal that assists a person with a physical or mental disability. That includes food, grooming, and veterinary care, claimed as an itemized medical expense on Schedule A.

There's a catch that trips people up: medical expenses only count once your total itemized medical costs exceed 7.5% of your adjusted gross income, and only the amount above that floor is deductible. A service animal has to be trained to perform specific tasks tied to a diagnosed disability, guiding a person who is blind, alerting someone to a medical episode, retrieving items for a person with limited mobility. An emotional support animal, however comforting, generally does not meet this standard on its own.

## Therapy dogs blur the line, but the test stays the same

Therapy dog deduction questions come up a lot, and the answer depends entirely on how the dog is used. If you run a for-profit therapy practice, a physical rehab business, or a similar service where the dog is integral to the service you deliver to paying clients, the dog's costs can qualify as an ordinary business expense under the same Publication 535 standard that covers guard dogs. The dog has to be doing real, income-generating work in the business, not just sitting in the waiting room.

A personal companion dog that happens to calm you down after a hard day doesn't qualify, no matter how therapeutic the relationship feels. The IRS applies the same business-purpose test here that it applies to a guard dog on a farm: is the animal actually performing work the business depends on, and can you prove it?

## What the IRS wants to see before you claim it

Before you claim any dog-related deduction, get in the habit of separating personal costs from business costs the same way you would for a vehicle used for both hauling grain and running errands. Keep vet bills, food receipts, and training invoices specific to the working animal. A short written note on the dog's role, the hours it works, and which entity it supports goes a long way if the IRS ever asks.

| Scenario | Deduction type | Where it's claimed | What's required |
|---|---|---|---|
| Guard dog protecting shop or inventory | Business expense | Schedule C or Schedule F | Documented business purpose, for-profit operation |
| Livestock guardian dog on a working farm | Business expense | Schedule F | Farm run for profit, dog tied to herd protection |
| Legitimate breeding operation | Business expense | Schedule C | Profit motive, records, business plan |
| Service dog for a diagnosed disability | Medical expense | Schedule A, itemized | Pub 502 standard, exceeds 7.5% AGI floor |
| Therapy dog used in a for-profit practice | Business expense | Schedule C | Dog integral to services sold to clients |
| Family pet or emotional support companion | Not deductible | N/A | Personal expense, no business or medical nexus |

## Common Questions

**Can I deduct my dog's food and vet bills if she guards our farm shop?**
Only if guarding the shop is a documented, ongoing part of a for-profit farm business filed on Schedule F, and only for the portion of her time and costs tied to that job. If she also lives in the house as a family pet, you need to separate personal use from business use before claiming anything.

**Is a therapy dog tax deductible?**
It depends entirely on the setting. A therapy dog used in a for-profit therapy or rehab practice can qualify as a business expense under IRS Publication 535, but a personal companion dog that provides comfort at home does not qualify, even with a doctor's note.

**What's the difference between a service animal and an emotional support animal for tax purposes?**
A service animal is trained to perform specific tasks for a diagnosed disability and qualifies as a medical expense under IRS Publication 502. An emotional support animal offers comfort but isn't trained to perform tasks, so it generally does not meet the medical expense test.

**Do I need to prorate expenses if my dog is part pet, part working animal?**
Yes. The IRS expects you to allocate costs based on actual business use, similar to how you'd split a vehicle used for both farm work and personal errands. Keep a simple log of hours worked and tie vet bills or training costs to the specific role whenever possible.
