A client walks in the first week of December with a folder of statements and one question: how much can we still do? Most years, the honest answer is not much. The moves that actually move the needle on a high-net-worth tax bill, shifting income across years, restructuring an entity, funding a charitable vehicle, take months to set up properly, not days. Waiting until the holidays to think about taxes is the single most expensive habit we see among successful individuals and business owners in Brookings and across South Dakota, Kingsbury, and Moody counties.

This isn't a listicle of tricks to try before December 31. It's a case for rethinking when tax planning happens at all.

## The December scramble doesn't save you money

By the time a calendar year is nearly over, most of the income has already happened. You can't retroactively change how a business sale was structured in March, or undo a large capital gain realized in June. Tax Topic 409 from the IRS lays out how [capital gains and losses](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc409) are taxed, and the rate you pay often depends on decisions made well before the sale, not after. Once the transaction is done, the tax treatment is largely locked in.

What's left in December is damage control: maxing out a retirement contribution, bunching a few deductions, maybe a last-minute equipment purchase. Useful, but small. These moves shave a few thousand dollars off a bill that proactive planning could have reduced by tens of thousands, sometimes more, if the groundwork had started in February.

## Wealthy individuals have more moving pieces, and more to lose

The more income sources you have, the more timing options exist, and the more it costs to ignore them. A farmer with grain sale proceeds, equipment depreciation, and land appreciation is managing a different tax picture than someone with a single W-2. An agribusiness operator running multiple entities, or a service business owner drawing both salary and distributions, has decisions to make in March, June, and September that shape what April looks like the following year.

We see this constantly with ag clients across the Midwest. A grain sale that could have been split across two tax years gets sold all at once because nobody looked at the whole picture until it was too late to change course. A piece of farmland gets sold without any planning around installment treatment. These aren't mistakes born of carelessness. They happen because tax planning got treated as a once-a-year event tied to a filing deadline instead of a year-round conversation.

## Multi-year income timing beats one-time deductions

The biggest dollar savings for high-net-worth individuals almost never come from a single clever deduction. They come from spreading income across tax years to stay out of the highest brackets, or timing a large gain to land in a year with offsetting losses or lower income elsewhere.

For a business owner selling a farm, a building, or a company, that means modeling the sale eighteen months out, not eighteen days out. For someone with concentrated stock or a large IRA, it means deciding in the spring whether a Roth conversion this year makes sense based on where income is likely to land by December, not guessing after the fact. None of this works if the first conversation with your CPA happens after Thanksgiving.

## Entity structure decisions need months, not days

How a farm, agribusiness, or service business is structured, sole proprietorship, partnership, S corp, or something else, affects self-employment tax, payroll requirements, and how much flexibility you have to time income. Changing structure isn't a form you file in December. It typically involves a new entity, new payroll setup, and state filings that need lead time to be done correctly and to actually hold up.

We've worked with agribusiness operators who saved five figures a year in self-employment tax simply by moving to an S corporation election, but only because the change was planned early enough in the year to be in place before most of the income arrived. Wait until fall, and the tax year is already mostly baked.

## Two strategies farmers and ag operators leave on the table

Two of the most overlooked opportunities for agriculture clients aren't exotic. They're just easy to miss without a CPA looking for them throughout the year.

The research and development credit isn't only for tech companies. Farmers experimenting with new seed varieties, irrigation techniques, soil amendments, or feed formulations may qualify for an R&D credit against activities most operators think of as just part of running the farm. Claiming it requires documenting the experimentation as it happens, which means it has to be tracked during the growing season, not reconstructed from memory the following April.

The excess fertility deduction is similarly missed. When farmland is purchased, a portion of the price may reflect fertilizer and nutrients already in the soil, above baseline levels, and that portion can potentially be deducted rather than capitalized into the land basis. Identifying and documenting that excess fertility takes a soil analysis and a conversation with your CPA close to the purchase date, not a scramble at filing time.

Both strategies share the same requirement: someone has to be looking for them before the year closes out, not after.

## Common Questions

**When should high-net-worth tax planning actually start each year?**
Ideally by the first quarter, so income timing, entity elections, and retirement or charitable strategies can be put in place before most of the year's income has already occurred. Waiting until Q4 limits you to smaller, reactive moves.

**Is the R&D credit really available to farmers?**
Yes, in many cases. Farmers experimenting with new seed varieties, soil amendments, feed formulations, or irrigation methods may qualify, but the experimentation needs to be documented as it happens during the growing season rather than reconstructed later.

**What is the excess fertility deduction and who qualifies?**
It's a deduction for the portion of a farmland purchase price attributable to fertilizer and nutrients already in the soil above baseline levels, rather than the land itself. It typically requires a soil analysis close to the purchase date and applies to farmland buyers, not general real estate purchases.

**Does proactive planning cost more than waiting until tax season?**
Not when it's structured as a bundled, fixed monthly relationship rather than hourly billing. The savings from earlier, well-timed decisions typically outweigh the planning cost many times over, especially for business owners with multiple income sources.
