If you've sat in a dealership finance office and watched the salesperson slide a lease quote across the desk right next to a loan quote, you already know the pitch: "Same payment, newer car, no worries." What they're not telling you is how the decision actually plays out on your tax return, your balance sheet, or your cash flow three years from now. That's the piece Julie Underwood, CPA, walks through with clients at TruCount CPA PC before they sign anything, whether it's a family sedan or a service truck for a Brookings-based contracting business.

The lease-versus-buy question isn't one-size-fits-all, and it shouldn't be answered by a payment calculator alone. Your mileage habits, whether the vehicle is used for business, and how you want to handle depreciation all change the math. Here's the framework we use with clients across Brookings, Kingsbury, and Moody counties, and how it applies whether you're buying a personal vehicle or adding a truck to an agribusiness fleet.

## What dealerships won't tell you about lease versus buy

A lease quote is built around residual value, not total cost. The dealership estimates what the car will be worth at the end of your term, subtracts that from the purchase price, and spreads the difference across your payments. That's why lease payments look smaller: you're only paying for the depreciation the dealer expects, plus interest (called a "money factor") and fees.

Buying means you're financing the entire purchase price, so your payment is higher, but you own something at the end. Interest rates matter a lot here. The Federal Reserve's [Consumer Credit (G.19) Report](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/g19/current/) tracks national auto loan trends, and even a one or two point swing in rates can shift the total cost of ownership by thousands of dollars over a five-year loan. Before you compare payments, compare the actual financing terms behind them.

## The cash flow math that matters more than the monthly payment

Monthly payment tunnel vision is the single biggest mistake we see, especially with business owners who are used to thinking in terms of "can I afford the payment" rather than "what does this cost me over the life of the vehicle."

Run the numbers over the full term, not just one month:

| Factor | Leasing | Buying |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly payment | Typically lower | Typically higher |
| Upfront cost | Lower or none | Down payment, taxes, fees |
| Mileage limits | Usually 10,000-15,000 miles/year | None |
| Ownership at end | None, unless you buy it out | Full ownership |
| Maintenance | Often covered under warranty | Your responsibility once warranty ends |
| Long-term cost | Higher if you lease repeatedly | Lower if you keep the vehicle 6+ years |

A farmer or agribusiness operator putting 25,000 miles a year on a pickup for field work is going to blow through lease mileage caps and pay steep overage fees, often 15 to 25 cents per mile. That same operator, buying the truck outright or financing it, avoids the penalty entirely and builds equity in an asset the operation actually uses every day.

## How Section 179 tips the scales for business vehicles

This is where the decision gets interesting for service-based businesses and agribusiness operations. If you buy and finance a vehicle used more than 50% for business, you may be able to deduct a significant portion of the purchase price in the year you place it in service under Section 179, rather than depreciating it slowly over five or six years.

Heavy vehicles, generally those with a gross vehicle weight rating over 6,000 pounds, like many pickups and cargo vans, qualify for a larger first-year deduction than passenger cars. The exact dollar limits change from year to year and depend on vehicle weight class and business-use percentage, so we always check current figures against [IRS Publication 946](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946) before finalizing a client's purchase timing. Lease payments, by contrast, are typically deducted as an ordinary business expense spread across the lease term. There's no upfront depreciation windfall.

For an agribusiness operator replacing a work truck this year, that difference can mean deducting a large chunk of the purchase price against this year's income instead of spreading a smaller deduction over several years. It's one reason we walk clients through the purchase timing conversation before harvest, not after, when there's still room to plan around it.

## Matching your vehicle choice to your driving habits and business use

Set the tax angle aside for a second. Buying almost always wins if any of these describe you:

- You drive more than 12,000-15,000 miles a year.
- You keep vehicles for 6 years or longer.
- You use the vehicle for farm or field work where dents, mud, and wear are unavoidable.
- You want to build equity instead of a recurring payment that never ends.

Leasing tends to make more sense if:

- You want a newer vehicle every 2-3 years and don't mind giving it back.
- Your mileage is predictable and stays under the lease cap.
- You'd rather keep cash flow steady with a lower payment and let the manufacturer absorb resale risk.
- The vehicle is for a service business where a professional, updated appearance matters, like a real estate agent's car or a consultant meeting clients regularly.

A lot of the service-based business owners we work with in Brookings fall into that second group for personal use, but flip to buying for their fleet or work vehicles once mileage and wear-and-tear enter the picture.

## What happens at the end of the lease or loan

This is the part dealership math conveniently skips. At the end of a loan, you have a paid-off asset with resale or trade-in value. At the end of a lease, you have three options: hand back the keys, buy it out at the predetermined residual value, or lease again. Buying out a lease can sometimes make sense if the residual value is lower than the vehicle's actual market value, but that's a calculation worth running with your CPA before the lease ends, not after you've already signed a new one.

For business vehicles, this end-of-term decision also affects your depreciation schedule and any Section 179 recapture if business use drops below 50% before the vehicle is fully depreciated. ## Common Questions

**Is it better to lease or buy a car for tax purposes?**
It depends on business use. If the vehicle is used more than 50% for business and you want a large first-year deduction, buying and financing typically wins under Section 179 rules outlined in [IRS Publication 946](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946). If the vehicle is mostly personal use, tax treatment is less of a factor and cash flow, mileage, and how long you keep vehicles should drive the decision.

**Can I deduct lease payments on a business vehicle?**
Yes. Lease payments on a vehicle used for business can generally be deducted as an ordinary business expense, prorated for business-use percentage, spread across the term of the lease rather than taken as a single upfront deduction.

**How does Section 179 work for a farm truck or work vehicle?**
Heavy vehicles over 6,000 pounds gross vehicle weight rating used primarily for farm or business purposes often qualify for a larger first-year deduction than passenger cars. The specific dollar limits change annually, so it's worth confirming current thresholds before finalizing a purchase.

**Should I buy out my lease at the end of the term?**
Buy it out if the residual value stated in your lease contract is lower than the vehicle's current market value or trade-in value. Otherwise, returning the vehicle and starting fresh, or buying a different vehicle outright, usually makes more financial sense.
