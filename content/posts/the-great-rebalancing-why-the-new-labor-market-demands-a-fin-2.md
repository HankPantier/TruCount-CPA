For two years, owners in Brookings and across the Midwest hired anyone who showed up, paid whatever it took to keep a crew intact through harvest, and treated a 15 percent raise as the cost of staying open. That era is easing. Quit rates are back near pre-2021 levels, applicant pools are deeper, and the frantic bidding wars for entry-level help have mostly stopped. But here's the part owners miss: the wages you raised in 2022 didn't come back down, and your payroll budget is still built on panic-era assumptions instead of a real plan. Labor cost management isn't an HR project anymore. It's a financial planning problem, and it belongs on the same table as your tax projections and your cash flow forecast.

This matters whether you're running 900 acres near Volga, managing a three-location ag retail operation, or billing hours out of a professional services office on Main Avenue. Labor is usually the largest controllable expense on the income statement, and it's the one most owners still manage by feel.

## Why a calmer labor market still costs you money

A cooling labor market doesn't mean cheaper labor. It means the emergency raises you handed out to stop the bleeding are now permanent, embedded in your base pay scale, and compounding every time you run an annual increase on top of them. A shop that bumped a $16 starting wage to $19 in 2022 to compete for hires isn't rolling that back now that applications are up. The new floor is $19, plus whatever cost-of-living bump gets layered on next year.

Meanwhile, benefits costs, workers' comp premiums, and payroll tax obligations scale with that higher wage base. So the real story of 2024 and 2025 isn't wage growth easing. It's wage growth easing while the elevated base from the last few years locks in as your new normal. If your budgeting still treats labor as "whatever it costs to fill the position," you're planning around a number that's already out of date the moment you write it down.

## What retention actually costs when you run the math

Most owners can tell you their turnover rate but not what it costs them. Run the numbers on a single departure: recruiting time, onboarding, lost productivity while a new hire ramps up, and overtime paid to cover the gap. For a skilled ag mechanic or an experienced bookkeeper, replacement cost commonly runs 20 to 30 percent of that role's annual pay once you count the ramp-up period, not just the ad spend and interview hours.

That reframes retention as a financial decision, not a culture initiative. A $2,000 retention bonus paid to a key seasonal crew lead who's stayed three years in a row is cheap compared to a spring scramble to find, train, and trust a stranger during planting. Professional service firms see the same math with client-facing staff: the cost of losing someone who knows your clients' history isn't just payroll, it's billable hours that don't get billed while someone new gets up to speed.

## Building labor cost into your books instead of your gut

Most small businesses track labor as one line: payroll expense. That tells you what you spent, not whether it's sustainable. A better approach breaks labor into a few numbers you actually watch monthly:

| Metric | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Labor cost as % of revenue | Whether pay is scaling with the business or outrunning it |
| Labor cost per unit (per acre, per head, per billable hour) | Whether efficiency is improving or slipping |
| Overtime as % of total payroll | Whether you're understaffed or mismanaging scheduling |
| Cost per hire vs. retention bonus cost | Whether it's cheaper to keep people or replace them |

For a grain operation, that might mean tracking labor cost per acre across planting, spraying, and harvest separately, since a $19-an-hour operator running a $400,000 combine during an eight-day harvest window is a very different cost decision than the same wage paid for winter maintenance work. For a professional service firm, it means comparing staff cost to realized billing rate by role, not just total payroll against total revenue.

When labor cost has its own line and its own trend, a wage decision stops being a gut call made in the break room and becomes a number you can actually plan a season around.

## Ag employers face a different version of the same problem

Seasonal and ag labor budgeting has its own wrinkles: overtime rules that don't apply the same way to agricultural workers, H-2A wage rate requirements that shift annually, and payroll that spikes hard for six weeks and disappears for six months. A crew of eight during harvest and two during January isn't a staffing plan, it's a cash flow event, and it needs to be modeled as one well before the first check gets cut.

## Turning this into a workforce budgeting strategy

A workforce budget worth having answers three questions before you post a job or hand out a raise: What does this role cost fully loaded, not just in base pay? What's the retention cost if this person leaves in year one versus year three? And what does labor need to look like as a percentage of revenue for the business to hit its margin target this year? The SBA's [guide to hiring and managing employees](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/manage-your-business/hire-manage-employees) is a solid starting point for the compliance side of this, covering classification, recordkeeping, and the basics of what the law requires as you add staff.

The financial side is where most owners get stuck, because it requires real-time numbers, not a spreadsheet updated once a year at tax time. That's the gap a bundled, fixed-fee accounting relationship is built to close. Instead of finding out in March that labor ran 34 percent of revenue instead of the 28 percent you budgeted, you see it drift in June, while there's still time to adjust hiring, pricing, or scheduling before it eats the year's profit.

## Common Questions

**Is wage growth actually slowing down for small businesses in 2024 and 2025?**
Hiring pressure has eased compared to 2021 and 2022, with more applicants per opening and fewer bidding wars for entry-level roles. But the wage increases made during the tight labor market mostly aren't reversing, so total labor cost stays elevated even as the pace of new increases slows.

**How do I know if my labor costs are too high?**
Compare labor cost as a percentage of revenue against your own trend over the last three years, not an industry average pulled from a blog. If that percentage is climbing while revenue growth is flat, the problem usually isn't wages, it's scheduling, overtime, or a staffing level built for a busier season than the one you're in.

**Does it make financial sense to pay a retention bonus instead of hiring someone new?**
Often yes, especially for roles where training time is long or client relationships matter. A retention bonus equal to 5 to 10 percent of annual pay is frequently cheaper than the 20 to 30 percent replacement cost tied to recruiting, onboarding, and lost productivity during a transition.

**Should ag employers budget labor differently than professional service businesses?**
Yes. Ag labor is seasonal and tied to weather-driven windows like planting and harvest, so cash flow planning needs to account for short, intense payroll spikes. Professional service firms usually carry more level staffing but need to track labor cost against billable hours and realized rate instead of raw revenue.
