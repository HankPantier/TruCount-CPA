Every dealership in Brookings will steer you toward the payment that feels smaller today. Every online calculator will tell you buying builds equity, full stop. Neither one knows your tax bracket, your annual mileage, or whether that vehicle sits on your Schedule C, your S-corp's books, or your personal checking account. That's the piece missing from most lease vs buy a car comparisons, and it's usually where the real money is.

## The question underneath the payment

For an individual buying a personal vehicle with zero business use, this decision mostly comes down to cash flow and how long you keep cars. Trade every three years and hate surprise repair bills? Leasing has real appeal. Drive a vehicle into the ground at 150,000 miles? Buying wins almost every time.

For a farm running a truck 40,000 miles a year hauling grain and livestock, or a professional service business titling a vehicle to the company, the calculation changes. Business vehicle purchase vs lease decisions carry tax consequences that can shift the total cost by thousands of dollars in the first year alone, before you even factor in what happens at trade-in.

## How the payment stacks up over five years

Here's a simplified look at a $45,000 crew cab truck, financed at 7% over 60 months versus leased on a comparable 36-month term.

| | Leasing | Buying with a loan |
|---|---|---|
| Typical monthly payment | Lower, often $550-650 | Higher, often $850-900 |
| Ownership at the end | None, unless you exercise a buyout | Full ownership |
| Mileage limits | 10,000-15,000 miles/year, overage fees apply | None |
| Maintenance | Often covered under factory warranty | Owner's responsibility once warranty ends |
| Business tax treatment | Deduct the business-use percentage of lease payments as paid | Depreciate the cost, possibly with Section 179 |

Over five years, that lease might run roughly $37,000 in total payments with nothing to show for it afterward. The loan runs closer to $53,000, but you're left holding a truck worth $15,000 to $20,000 at trade-in. Run the numbers on your specific vehicle and mileage before assuming either side wins by default.

## Section 179 tips the scale for business vehicles

If a vehicle is used more than 50% for business, buying opens a door leasing doesn't: accelerated depreciation. Under the rules outlined in [IRS Publication 946](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946), heavy vehicles, generally those over 6,000 pounds gross vehicle weight, qualify for a substantially larger first-year Section 179 vehicle deduction than passenger cars, which are capped under separate luxury-auto depreciation limits.

Say a farmer buys a $55,000 crew cab used 80% for hauling feed and equipment. Depending on the year's limits and the owner's tax bracket, that Section 179 election could reduce the current-year tax bill by $8,000 to $12,000. Lease that same truck and you only deduct the business-use portion of the payments as they're made, spread thin over the lease term instead of taken up front. The tradeoff: buying locks you into ownership and depreciation recapture rules if you sell early, so this is a decision worth modeling with your CPA before you sign anything, not after.

Entity structure matters here too. A vehicle titled to a farm LLC versus held personally changes how the deduction flows through, which is part of why [entity type analysis](/what-we-do/advisory-services/entity-type-analysis) tends to come up in the same conversation as vehicle purchases.

## Mileage habits decide more than the tax code does

A farmer or ag operator logging 30,000 to 50,000 miles a year on rural roads and gravel, hauling trailers and equipment, will blow past lease mileage caps almost every year. Overage charges commonly run $0.15 to $0.25 per mile, which turns a good-looking lease payment into an expensive mistake by year two.

Compare that to a professional service business owner driving under 10,000 miles a year for client meetings around Brookings. Lower mileage, lighter wear, and a preference for a newer vehicle every few years can make leasing the more sensible pick, especially when the vehicle isn't central to how the business earns revenue.

## What this looks like for farmers, agribusiness, and service businesses

Farm and agribusiness operations usually lean toward buying. Heavy trucks, high annual mileage, and Section 179 eligibility stack in favor of ownership, and the equipment often outlasts the loan term by years. This is a regular part of the [tax preparation and planning](/what-we-do/outsourced-accounting/business-tax) conversations we have with row crop and livestock operations across South Dakota.

Professional service businesses, from consulting firms to real estate offices, tend to have more flexibility. Lower mileage and less wear-and-tear make leasing viable, particularly for owners who value predictable payments over long-term ownership. If the vehicle is a minor part of overall business assets, the simplicity of leasing can outweigh the tax advantage of buying. It's worth reviewing this alongside your broader [accounting services for service-based businesses](/who-we-serve/professional-service-businesses) rather than deciding on the vehicle in isolation.

## Run the cash flow test before you sign

Interest rates on auto loans move with the broader credit market, and the [Federal Reserve's Consumer Credit (G.19) report](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/g19/current/) tracks how those rates shift over time. A loan that penciled out favorably eighteen months ago may not today, which is a good reason to run the actual after-tax cost of both options before committing to financing over leasing, or the reverse.

The monthly payment is the easiest number to compare and the least useful one on its own. What matters is the after-tax cost per mile driven, factoring in depreciation, interest, maintenance, and how the vehicle is titled. That's a five-minute conversation with a CPA who already knows your entity structure and tax situation, not a spreadsheet you build alone the night before you sign.

## Common Questions

**Is leasing or buying better for a small business vehicle?**
It depends on mileage and business-use percentage. High-mileage, heavy-use vehicles like farm trucks usually favor buying because of Section 179 eligibility and no mileage caps. Lower-mileage vehicles for client meetings or light delivery often lease more affordably, especially if you replace vehicles every few years.

**Can I deduct lease payments on my taxes?**
Yes, but only the business-use percentage. If you use a leased vehicle 70% for business, you deduct 70% of the lease payment along with 70% of related operating costs like fuel and insurance, based on mileage logs that support that percentage.

**What's the Section 179 vehicle deduction limit?**
Limits vary by year and vehicle weight class, with heavier vehicles (over 6,000 pounds gross vehicle weight) eligible for a much larger first-year deduction than passenger cars. Details are outlined in [IRS Publication 946](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946), and current-year limits should be confirmed before you purchase.

**Does a farm truck qualify for Section 179?**
Most farm trucks used over 50% for business qualify, provided the business-use percentage is documented. Weight class affects how much you can deduct in year one, which is why crew cab and heavy-duty trucks common on South Dakota operations often see the largest first-year benefit.
