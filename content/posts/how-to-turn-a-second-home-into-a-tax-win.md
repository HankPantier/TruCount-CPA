Buying a cabin at Lake Poinsett or a place in the Black Hills sounds like a lifestyle decision. For the IRS, it's a math problem. The number of days you personally use that property versus the number of days you rent it out determines whether your second home works like a tax shelter, a rental business, or something in between. Get the days wrong and you could lose deductions you assumed were automatic, or trigger income reporting you didn't expect.

Most second-home advice out there comes from real estate agents talking about resale value and mortgage rates. That's useful, but it skips the part that actually affects your tax bill: how the IRS classifies the property based on your use. Here's how that classification works and what it means for your return.

## Three tax categories, one house

The IRS doesn't ask whether you call it a vacation home, a cabin, or a rental. It looks at two numbers: personal-use days and rental days. Those numbers sort your property into one of three buckets under [IRS Publication 527](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527).

| Situation | Personal use | Rental use | How it's taxed |
|---|---|---|
| Personal residence with minimal rental | Any amount | 14 days or fewer | Rental income isn't reported at all; no rental deductions |
| Dwelling used as a home | More than 14 days, or more than 10% of rental days | Any amount | Rental income reported; expenses limited to rental income, no loss allowed |
| Rental property | 14 days or fewer, or 10% or less of rental days | Any amount | Rental income reported; losses may be deductible, subject to passive activity rules |

That middle category catches more second-home owners than people realize, especially in a place like Brookings where a lake property might get used heavily by family in July and August, then rented out sparingly the rest of the year.

## The 14-day rule that lets you skip reporting entirely

If you rent your second home for 14 days or fewer during the year, you don't report the rental income at all, no matter how much you charged. Rent your Lake Poinsett cabin for a weekend during Hobo Day and pocket $1,800? That income never touches your Form 1040. This is sometimes called the Augusta rule, and it applies to any residence you use personally, not just the one you live in year-round.

The tradeoff: you also can't deduct any rental-related expenses, since the IRS treats the home as fully personal for the year. No depreciation, no prorated utilities, no cleaning fees written off. Fourteen days and under is a clean, simple exception, but it only works in your favor if the rental income itself is the goal, not the deductions.

## Second home vs investment property, and why the label matters

"Second home" and "investment property" get used interchangeably in real estate conversations, but the IRS treats them very differently, and the distinction comes down to that personal-use threshold again.

A second home where you spend more than 14 days a year, or more than 10% of the days it's rented, is a dwelling used as a home under Section 280A. Mortgage interest on that property can still be deductible on Schedule A, subject to the $750,000 combined mortgage debt limit across all your homes. But if you rent it out, your rental deductions are capped at your rental income. You can zero out the rental income with expenses, but you can't create a loss to offset your other income.

Drop your personal use to 14 days or fewer (or 10% of rental days, whichever is greater), and the property becomes a rental property in the IRS's eyes. Now you're on Schedule E, mortgage interest and property taxes get allocated to the rental side, and losses become possible. Real losses, the kind that can offset other income, subject to passive activity loss rules and a $25,000 special allowance for active participants that phases out between $100,000 and $150,000 of adjusted gross income.

## A concrete example using local numbers

Say a Brookings couple buys a cabin near Oakwood Lakes for $240,000. They use it 20 days themselves and rent it 40 days for a total of $9,600.

Because 20 personal-use days is more than 14, and more than 10% of 40 rental days (4 days), the cabin is a dwelling used as a home. They report the $9,600 as rental income, deduct prorated mortgage interest, property taxes, insurance, and maintenance up to that $9,600, and stop there. No loss, even if their actual prorated expenses ran to $12,000.

Now flip it. Same cabin, but they only use it 10 days themselves and rent it 55 days for $13,200. Personal use of 10 days is below both the 14-day threshold and 10% of 55 rental days. The cabin is now a rental property. Depreciation enters the picture, prorated expenses can exceed rental income, and if the couple actively manages the booking and maintenance themselves, they may be able to deduct up to $25,000 of any resulting loss against their other income, depending on their AGI.

Ten days of personal use is the difference between a capped deduction and real tax-reducing losses. That's not a footnote. That's the whole ballgame, and it's exactly the kind of number most buyers don't track until it's too late to fix for the year.

## What you can actually deduct once it's a rental

Once personal use falls under the threshold and the property qualifies as a rental, deductible expenses typically include:

- Mortgage interest and property taxes, prorated for rental days
- Homeowners insurance, prorated
- Utilities and HOA dues, prorated
- Repairs and routine maintenance
- Property management or cleaning fees tied to rental turnover
- Depreciation on the rental-use portion of the property

Everything gets split between personal and rental use based on the day count, which means the calendar you keep matters as much as the receipts. [IRS Tax Topic 415](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc415) walks through how the allocation works when a property serves both purposes in the same year.

## Common Questions

**What is the IRS 14-day rule for a second home?**
If you rent your second home for 14 days or fewer during the tax year, you don't have to report the rental income at all, regardless of the amount collected. In exchange, you can't deduct any rental-related expenses for that year, since the IRS treats the property as entirely personal use.

**Can I deduct a loss on a second home I rent out?**
Only if the property qualifies as a rental under IRS rules, meaning your personal use is 14 days or fewer, or no more than 10% of the days it's rented, whichever is greater. If personal use exceeds that threshold, your rental deductions are capped at your rental income and no loss is allowed.

**Is a second home the same as an investment property for tax purposes?**
No. A second home you use personally more than 14 days a year (or more than 10% of rental days) is treated as a dwelling used as a home, with limited rental deductions. An investment property with minimal personal use follows different rules, including depreciation and potential loss deductions under passive activity limits.

**Do I need to report rental income if I only rent my cabin out occasionally?**
If total rental days for the year are 14 or fewer, no, that income is exempt from reporting under the 14-day rule. Once you cross 15 rental days, all rental income becomes reportable, and your deductions depend on how many days you personally used the property that same year.
