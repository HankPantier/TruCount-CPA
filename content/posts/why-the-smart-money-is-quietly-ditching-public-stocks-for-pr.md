A handful of clients have asked us the same question this year. A wealth manager mentioned private credit or a private equity fund, and now they want to know what it actually does to their tax return. It's a fair question, and it's happening for a reason: the pool of companies trading on public exchanges keeps shrinking, and money that used to chase IPOs is now chasing negotiated deals nobody sees on a stock ticker.

## Fewer IPOs are changing where the money goes

Fewer companies choose to go public than they did fifteen years ago. Staying private longer means avoiding the disclosure costs, activist shareholders, and quarterly earnings pressure that come with a listing. For investors, that used to mean fewer options. No shares on an exchange meant no exposure. Now private equity funds, direct lending vehicles, and private credit funds have opened a lane that used to be reserved for institutions and family offices with eight-figure minimums.

That lane is showing up in ordinary conversations around Brookings. A farmer who sells 320 acres of appreciated land, or a professional service owner who just closed a practice sale, is increasingly getting pitched a private credit fund with a quarterly capital call instead of a mutual fund with same-day liquidity. The pitch usually leads with the return. It rarely leads with the K-1.

## Higher rates made private, negotiated yield hard to ignore

When rates sat near zero, the yield gap between public bonds and private credit wasn't worth the illiquidity. That gap widened once rates moved up. Private credit funds negotiate covenants, collateral, and rate resets loan by loan, an advantage a public bondholder simply doesn't have. The Federal Reserve's [Financial Stability Report](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/financial-stability-report.htm) has tracked this shift for several reporting cycles now, flagging private credit's growth as a factor worth watching for the financial system broadly, not just for portfolio returns.

For an individual investor, that growth translates into more offers landing in the inbox. Insurance-linked private credit, direct lending funds, real estate debt funds, and ag-focused lending vehicles have all expanded their investor base beyond pension funds and endowments. The return story is often genuinely attractive. The tax and cash-flow story is the part that gets skipped.

## The K-1 complexity nobody mentions at the pitch meeting

A public stock is simple at tax time. You get a 1099-B, you know your cost basis, you report a capital gain or loss, and you're usually done by February. A private fund issues a K-1, and K-1s from multi-state, multi-strategy funds routinely arrive in March, June, or even September of the following year.

Three issues show up almost every time:

| Issue | What it means for you |
|---|---|
| Timing | K-1s often arrive after the original filing deadline, requiring an extension even though tax is still owed in April |
| Phantom income | The fund can allocate taxable income to you before it distributes any cash |
| State footprint | A fund investing across ten states can require you to file ten state returns, even on a modest allocation |

None of these are reasons to avoid private markets. They're reasons to plan for them before the wire goes out, not after the K-1 shows up in a mailbox six months later than expected.

## Where this shows up for Brookings ag and business owners

Farm and agribusiness clients already deal with multi-entity structures: an operating LLC, a land-holding entity, maybe an equipment leasing entity, each generating its own K-1. Layer a private credit or private equity allocation on top of that, and a producer who used to have three K-1s now has four or five, each with its own timing and its own state-filing quirks. Julie Underwood, CPA, works with clients on exactly this kind of [multi-entity farm tax strategy](/industries/farmers/multi-entity-farm-tax-strategy), and the first question she asks before an allocation closes is whether the client's estimated tax payments already account for income the fund hasn't distributed yet.

Professional service business owners run into a related version of the same problem. A retirement plan rollover or a brokerage account that adds a private equity sleeve can trigger unrelated business taxable income inside an IRA, a bill that surprises people who assumed a retirement account was tax-sheltered by definition. It isn't, automatically, once leverage or an active trade enters the picture.

## Build the tax plan before you sign, not after

The fix isn't complicated, but it has to happen on a timeline that matches how these funds actually operate. Before capital gets committed, we look at three things: the fund's expected state filing footprint, whether the strategy is likely to generate phantom income in early years, and how that income should factor into quarterly estimated payments for the current tax year. That analysis takes an afternoon when it happens ahead of the commitment. It takes a scramble in April when it doesn't.

This is the same reasoning behind why [waiting until December to plan](/resources/the-biggest-tax-mistake-wealthy-individuals-make-waiting-unt) costs high-net-worth individuals real money: private market allocations are a decision with tax consequences that stretch years past the initial signature, and the earlier those consequences get modeled, the fewer surprises land on the return. It's also part of why a portfolio built on [set it and forget it](/resources/strategically-balancing-your-investment-portfolio-why-set-it) thinking tends to underperform once illiquid holdings enter the mix. Illiquid assets don't rebalance themselves, and neither does the tax plan around them.

## Common Questions

**What's the main tax difference between investing in public stocks versus private equity or private credit funds?**
Public stock gains are straightforward: a 1099-B, a cost basis, a capital gain or loss, usually finalized by February. Private funds issue K-1s that often arrive in March or later, can report taxable income before you receive any cash, and may require filings in every state the fund does business.

**What is UBTI and why does it matter inside a retirement account?**
Unrelated business taxable income is income a tax-exempt account, like an IRA, earns when a fund it holds uses leverage or runs an active trade or business. Once UBTI exceeds $1,000 in a year, the IRA itself owes tax on Form 990-T, a bill many investors don't expect a retirement account to generate.

**How long do K-1s take to arrive, and can I still file on time?**
Many private fund K-1s arrive between March and September of the year after the tax year closes. Filing a timely extension is standard practice for these investments, and any tax owed is still due by the original April deadline even though the paperwork isn't ready yet.

**Does a private credit or private equity investment change my estimated tax payments?**
Often, yes. If the fund allocates income without distributing matching cash, you may owe estimated tax on money you haven't actually received. We calculate that exposure as soon as fund estimates are available so a September K-1 doesn't turn into an April penalty.
