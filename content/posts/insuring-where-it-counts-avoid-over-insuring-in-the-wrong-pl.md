Ask five people how much insurance coverage you need and you'll get five different rules of thumb: ten times your salary in life insurance, full replacement cost on every building, a flat $1 million in liability because that's what everyone else carries. None of those numbers come from anything specific to your farm, your service business, or your family's actual finances. They're guesses dressed up as guidance. The better question isn't which policies to buy. It's how much financial exposure you actually have, and how much of it you can afford to absorb yourself before insurance needs to cover the rest.

This conversation comes up at TruCount CPA PC more often through tax planning than through insurance shopping. A client's premiums jump, their equipment list grows, or they hire a second employee, and the coverage they bought five years ago no longer matches the business sitting in front of them. Some parts end up underinsured. Other parts sit quietly overinsured, draining cash every month for protection nobody needs. Julie Underwood, CPA, and the team have watched both mistakes cost real money for farmers in Kingsbury and Moody counties and service business owners across Brookings, SD.

## Insurance is a math problem, not a shopping list

Every insurance decision comes down to one calculation: what's the dollar cost if this specific bad thing happens, and what's the annual cost to transfer that risk to an insurer instead of carrying it yourself. A grain bin fire, a lawsuit from a slip-and-fall at a client's office, a combine operator hurt on the job, a key employee who quits mid-season. Each of those has a real dollar figure attached, even if it's uncomfortable to estimate.

Once you know that number, the coverage decision gets simpler. If losing a piece of equipment worth $40,000 wouldn't meaningfully change your balance sheet, self-insuring that risk with a higher deductible might make more sense than paying a premium to eliminate it entirely. If losing your only combine during harvest would stop cash flow for months, that's a different math problem entirely, and it deserves real coverage, not the cheapest quote you found online.

## Start with what you can't afford to lose

Net worth is the anchor for this entire exercise. A farmer with 800 acres, $3.2 million in land and equipment, and $180,000 in operating debt has a very different exposure profile than a service business owner with $150,000 in annual revenue, one employee, and a home office. The farmer's liability exposure is tied to land, equipment, and hired labor. The service business owner's exposure is tied more to professional liability, a single-person income stream, and the cash flow gap if that one employee is out for eight weeks.

A reasonable personal umbrella policy for someone with $1.5 million in liquid and fixed net worth looks different from one for someone carrying $300,000. The umbrella should extend coverage past where your homeowners or auto liability limits stop, not duplicate what's already there. That's the piece most people get wrong: they buy an umbrella policy without checking whether it actually starts where their underlying policies end, and they end up paying for a coverage gap they never closed.

## Where over-insuring quietly drains cash flow

Over-insuring rarely looks dramatic. It looks like a $500 deductible on a policy where a $2,500 deductible would cut the premium by 15% and the business has enough cash reserve to absorb the difference without blinking. It looks like life insurance sized to replace gross income for thirty years when the mortgage is paid off in twelve and the kids are through college in eight. It looks like carrying full comprehensive and collision on a truck that's worth less than the annual premium.

Here's a simple comparison that shows how this plays out for two different clients:

| Coverage type | Common default | Right-sized based on exposure |
|---|---|---|
| Auto/equipment deductible | $500 | $2,500, backed by 3 months of cash reserve |
| Life insurance | 10x salary, flat | Income replacement gap plus outstanding debt, minus liquid assets |
| Business liability | $1 million, standard | Matched to largest single asset or contract exposure |
| Personal umbrella | Skipped or under-limits | Starts where auto/home liability ends, covers net worth above $500,000 |

The deductible decision alone is worth revisiting every year. Raising deductibles across a farm's property and equipment policies by even $1,000 per item can free up several hundred dollars a year in premium, money that's better used building the cash reserve that makes the higher deductible safe to carry in the first place. ## A risk-based approach for agribusiness and service businesses

Farmers and agribusiness operators carry a specific set of exposures that generic insurance content doesn't address well: crop and hail coverage sized to actual planted acres and current commodity prices, equipment coverage that keeps pace with the replacement cost of a combine or planter (which has climbed sharply in the last five years), and liability coverage for hired labor during planting and harvest.

Service businesses have a different profile. Professional liability, sometimes called errors and omissions coverage, protects against claims tied to advice or work product, not physical property. A single-person consulting practice with $150,000 in revenue doesn't need the same property coverage as a farm operation, but it may need more liability protection relative to its size, since a lawsuit could wipe out several years of profit in one claim.

Both groups tend to overlook business interruption coverage until they need it. If a fire or equipment failure stops operations for six weeks, business interruption coverage replaces lost income during that window, not just the cost to repair or replace the damaged property. That's a gap worth closing before it becomes a crisis, not after.

## Running the numbers before you buy

A proper insurance needs analysis starts with three numbers: your liquid net worth, your fixed net worth, and your annual cash flow after debt service. From there, you can work out how much of a given loss you could absorb without borrowing or liquidating assets, and how much needs to be transferred to an insurer.

This is also where insurance decisions intersect with tax planning. Business insurance premiums are generally deductible as an ordinary business expense, as outlined in [IRS Publication 535](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p535), which means the after-tax cost of a policy is often lower than the sticker price suggests. That doesn't mean more coverage is automatically the right call, but it does change the math when you're weighing a higher premium against a higher deductible.

## Common Questions

**How much insurance coverage do I actually need?**
There's no universal percentage. Start by calculating your liquid net worth, your fixed assets at risk, and your annual cash flow after debt payments. Coverage should fill the gap between what a major loss would cost and what you could absorb without borrowing or selling assets.

**Do I need business interruption insurance for a farm or service business?**
If a fire, equipment failure, or extended illness would stop your income for more than a few weeks, business interruption coverage is worth pricing out. It replaces lost income during the shutdown, which standard property coverage doesn't do.

**Is business insurance tax deductible?**
Generally yes. Business insurance premiums are typically deductible as an ordinary and necessary business expense under IRS guidelines outlined in [Publication 535](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p535). The specifics depend on the policy type and how the business is structured.

**How often should I review my coverage?**
At least once a year, and any time your asset base, revenue, or employee count changes meaningfully. A combine purchase, a new hire, or a jump in land value are all good triggers for a fresh review rather than waiting for renewal season.
