A lot of insurance shopping happens backward. An agent hands over a renewal packet, the owner skims the declarations page, and the policy gets renewed because last year's numbers looked fine. Nobody asks the actual question: if this specific risk happened tomorrow, what would it cost, and could the business absorb that cost without insurance? That question, not a generic checklist of policy types, is what should drive every coverage decision.

We see this constantly in Brookings and across the farms and service businesses we work with in South Dakota. A farm operation insures a fifteen-year-old grain truck at full replacement cost while carrying no coverage at all for the six weeks of lost income if the one person who runs the planter gets hurt. A law firm or engineering shop pays for a general liability policy sized to match what a competitor down the street carries, without ever calculating what a six-month interruption would actually cost in lost billings. Insurance bought this way isn't protection. It's a guess dressed up as a plan.

## Insurance is a math problem before it's a shopping list

Every risk you carry falls into one of two buckets: things your business or personal balance sheet can absorb on its own, and things that would force you to borrow, liquidate assets, or shut down operations. Insurance only makes financial sense for the second bucket. If a loss would cost $8,000 and you have $50,000 sitting in an operating reserve, paying a premium to transfer that risk is often money spent solving a problem you already solved yourself.

The calculation gets more useful once you frame it around two numbers: your net worth (what you'd lose access to in a worst-case claim) and your monthly cash flow (what you need coming in to keep operating while a claim gets sorted out). A farm with $3 million in land equity and $40,000 in monthly operating expenses has a very different risk profile than a two-partner CPA firm with $200,000 in retained earnings and $60,000 in monthly payroll. Both need coverage. Neither needs the same coverage.

## Where farm and agribusiness owners tend to over-insure

Equipment is the easiest place to overspend. Farmers often insure aging machinery at full replacement value years after the actual liquidation or resale value has dropped well below that number. A combine purchased for $420,000 a decade ago isn't worth $420,000 today, and insuring it as if it were means paying premiums calculated against a number that no longer exists.

The same pattern shows up with duplicate coverage across entities. Multi-entity farm structures, where land, equipment, and the operating business sit in separate LLCs or partnerships for tax reasons, sometimes end up with the same asset insured twice because two entities each carry a policy that references it. If your operation runs through more than one entity for tax purposes, it's worth checking coverage alongside the entity structure itself. That's the kind of overlap we catch when we work through multi-entity farm tax strategy with clients, and it's often the first place real premium savings show up.

## Where professional service business owners tend to under-insure

The opposite problem shows up in service businesses. A firm carries a standard $1 million general liability policy because that's what the landlord's lease requires or what a client contract specifies, and stops there. What's often missing is coverage for the risk that actually threatens the business: losing the person who brings in most of the revenue.

In a four-person accounting or engineering firm, one partner often accounts for 40 to 60 percent of billable revenue. If that person is disabled or dies unexpectedly, the firm doesn't just lose a colleague. It loses cash flow at the exact moment it needs cash to cover a buyout, hire a replacement, or wind down client relationships. Key person insurance and buy-sell funding exist specifically for this gap, and they're the pieces most often missing from a professional service business's coverage, not the general liability limit everyone already has.

## A four-part exposure check worth running every year

A useful annual review breaks risk into four categories and asks a specific question in each one.

| Risk category | Question to ask | Common miss |
|---|---|---|
| Property | What's the current liquidation value, not the original purchase price? | Insuring depreciated equipment at replacement cost |
| Liability | What's the realistic maximum claim size given your revenue and client base? | Coverage limits set by habit, not exposure |
| Income replacement | How many months could operations run without your top revenue producer or a key piece of equipment? | No business interruption or key person coverage |
| Succession and transfer | Does a death or disability trigger a cash need the business can't cover on its own? | No buy-sell agreement funded by life insurance |

Running through this table takes less than an hour once the numbers are in front of you. Most owners have never had all four columns filled in at the same time, which is exactly why gaps and overlaps both go unnoticed for years.

## How coverage choices show up on your tax return

Premiums for most business insurance, property, liability, and workers' compensation among them, are generally deductible as ordinary business expenses under [IRS Publication 535](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p535). Life insurance premiums for key person policies typically are not deductible when the business is the beneficiary, which changes the real after-tax cost of that coverage compared to a property or liability policy with the same premium. Comparing two policies by premium alone, without accounting for deductibility, is comparing the wrong numbers.

This is where a coverage review overlaps with tax planning rather than staying a pure insurance conversation. It also overlaps with succession work: a farm operation planning a transition to the next generation, or a professional service business mapping out an eventual sale, needs its insurance and its transition plan built around the same net worth figures. We walk clients through this as part of [farm succession planning](/industries/farmers/farm-succession-planning) and [service business exit planning](/industries/professional-service-businesses/exit-planning), because a funding gap discovered mid-transition is a lot more expensive to fix than one caught during an annual review.

## Common Questions

**How much insurance coverage do you need for a farm or small business?**
There's no flat number. Start by identifying your maximum realistic loss in each risk category, then compare it against what your business could absorb from cash reserves without borrowing. Coverage should close the gap between those two figures, not simply match what a competitor or landlord requires.

**What's the difference between insuring for replacement value and insuring for actual exposure?**
Replacement value covers what it costs to buy something new. Exposure-based coverage accounts for what you'd actually lose, including lost income and liquidation value, which is usually lower for equipment and higher for income disruption than most policies assume.

**Is business insurance tax deductible?**
Most property, liability, and workers' compensation premiums are deductible as ordinary business expenses under [IRS Publication 535](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p535). Key person life insurance where the business is the beneficiary generally is not, which affects the real cost of that coverage.

**When does key person insurance make sense for a professional service business?**
It makes sense when one person accounts for a large share of revenue or client relationships, commonly 30 percent or more in small firms. The policy should be sized to cover the cash gap a sudden loss would create, including buyout funding and the cost of replacing that revenue while a successor gets up to speed.
