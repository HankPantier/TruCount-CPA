## Protect your family farm's future with a clear transition plan

Passing a farm from one generation to the next is rarely just about paperwork. It's the 6 a.m. conversations at the kitchen table, the son or daughter who's spent a decade in the field wondering if the operation will be theirs, and the parents who built something over 30 years and want to see it land in good hands.

At TruCount CPA PC, we've sat with farm families across Brookings, Kingsbury, and Moody counties who needed more than an estate document filed away in a drawer. Julie Underwood, CPA, and the TruCount team work through the financial mechanics behind those family conversations, so a succession plan holds up whether the harvest is good or the markets turn hard.

A plan built once and never revisited tends to fall apart the moment land values shift or an heir's plans change. That's why succession planning here isn't a single meeting. It's a relationship that keeps pace with your operation as it grows and changes hands.

## Why farm succession planning in South Dakota requires specialized expertise

South Dakota farm families face a layer of complexity a standard estate plan doesn't touch. Land values have climbed sharply over the past decade, and how that ground is titled, whether through an LLC, a trust, or joint tenancy, changes what heirs owe and how easily an operation keeps running through a transition. Equipment depreciation schedules, crop share arrangements, and multi-entity farm and ranch structures each carry tax consequences specific to production agriculture.

SDSU Extension's Sustaining the Legacy conferences do valuable work introducing farm families to estate planning basics, and a good estate attorney drafts the legal documents a transition requires. Neither typically builds the ongoing financial models a multi-generation farm needs: cash flow projections under a new ownership structure, tax liability estimates for a phased buyout, or entity comparisons that hold up over a 10-year window. That modeling work belongs to a CPA who knows agriculture, not just accounting.

## Structuring your farm and agribusiness entities for a smooth transition

Most farm operations in South Dakota aren't one business, they're a collection of entities: a land-holding LLC, an equipment company, a cattle operation, maybe a trucking or custom application business layered on top. How those pieces are structured determines who can buy in, how ownership transfers without triggering a tax bill nobody planned for, and whether the next generation inherits flexibility or a mess.

TruCount's [entity type analysis](/services/entity-type-analysis) looks at the full operation, not one piece of it, and maps out how a corporation, LLC, or partnership structure affects the transition you're planning. For families running a farm alongside a separate service business, we also coordinate strategy through our [multi-entity farm tax approach](/industries/agriculture/multi-entity-farm-tax-strategy) so decisions on one side don't undercut the tax plan on the other. Get the structure right early, and the ownership transfer itself becomes a formality instead of a scramble.

## Tax strategies that preserve wealth across generations

Farm transitions create tax exposure at nearly every step: gifting land to an heir, structuring a buyout, retiring a partner's equity. A handful of strategies get overlooked because they're specific to production agriculture and easy to miss if your advisor isn't looking for them.

- Gifting strategies that move land or equity to the next generation within annual and lifetime exclusion limits
- Buyout structures that spread the outgoing generation's payout over years to ease cash flow on both sides
- The excess fertility deduction, which lets qualifying farmland sales account for soil nutrient value most families never claim
- The R&D credit for farmers, applicable to activities like new seed variety testing, irrigation improvements, and process changes many operations don't realize qualify
- Entity-level tax elections that change how a sale or transfer is taxed depending on structure

These get built into a multi-year tax plan, not a single filing season, so the family captures savings before a transition locks in a less favorable structure.

## Working through buyout and retirement scenarios with confidence

Retirement looks different for a farmer than for most business owners. There's no clean exit date, and the outgoing generation often needs the farm's income to keep flowing even after ownership changes hands. TruCount builds financial models that show what retirement actually looks like: what a buyout payment schedule does to cash flow, what the incoming generation can realistically service in debt, and where Social Security, rental income, or a phased consulting arrangement fills the gap.

Sitting down with these numbers in front of everyone at the table changes the conversation. Instead of guessing what's fair, both generations see the same projections and negotiate from shared facts. That's often the difference between a transition that holds together and one that ends up in a dispute.

**Q: What is the excess fertility deduction and does it apply to my farm?**
A: The excess fertility deduction allows qualifying farmland sales to account for residual soil nutrient value, reducing taxable gain. It's frequently overlooked in farm transitions. TruCount reviews whether a sale or transfer during succession qualifies for this deduction.
